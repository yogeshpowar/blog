Building has become fast, and it will keep getting faster. Failure has
become cheap in time, and teams that use that to learn will pull ahead. But
nothing about running a platform, keeping it secure, satisfying the rules
it lives under, or finding people who want what it offers has become cheap.
If anything, those costs have grown, because there is more software to own
and the foundations under it are less settled than they were.

Every team now sits between those two facts, and no position on the line
is free. The orthodox team pays in speed and in the ideas it never tests.
The fully agentic team pays in decisions nobody made and evidence nobody
can show. The agent-skeptic team pays a little of both while feeling it
has paid neither. Every team is choosing. The only question is whether it
**chooses on purpose**.

None of this removes the need for engineers. It changes what good
engineering looks like. The engineer who writes a clear map, reads
evidence carefully, owns an environment, and knows when to say "we should
not build this" is more valuable now, not less. I do not know how teams
will look in five years, and I am wary of anyone who claims to. What I do
know is that judgement about what matters, and accountability for it,
still sit with people. Tasks can be assigned, to a person or to an agent,
and so can authority. As I wrote in [From Character to
Ownership]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-07-22-ownership %}),
ownership cannot.

So I come back to the decision that agents help with least. When almost
anything can be built by Friday, the scarce skill is not building. It is
choosing. Most of what a team could build will look right, and from the
inside the idea that will quietly fail looks just like the rest. The work,
especially for leaders, is to test what lies outside the code before the
building starts, and to keep the evidence for every yes and every no.

The most valuable decision is often **what not to build**. Agents have not
changed that. They have made it matter more.
